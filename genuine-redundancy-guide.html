Genuine Redundancy Guide

What makes a redundancy “genuine” under the Fair Work Act

Not every dismissal labelled "redundancy" actually qualifies as one under the Fair Work Act. Here's the legal test, and what it means if your employer hasn't followed it.

The three-part legal test

Under the Fair Work Act, a redundancy is only "genuine" if it meets three conditions, all at once: the employer no longer requires the person's job to be done by anyone, because of changes in the operational requirements of the business; the employer has complied with any consultation obligations in an applicable award or enterprise agreement; and it would not have been reasonable in the circumstances to redeploy the employee elsewhere in the business, or an associated entity. If any one of these three isn't met, the redundancy may not be genuine in the legal sense — regardless of what it's called on your termination letter.

Consultation requirements

Most modern awards and enterprise agreements require an employer to notify and consult affected employees as soon as a definite decision to make workplace changes likely to significantly affect staff has been made — not after the fact. This typically means discussing the changes, their likely effects on employees, and ways to minimise negative impact, such as redeployment or voluntary redundancy. Skipping this step, or presenting the decision as already final with no real consultation, is one of the most common grounds employees raise when disputing a redundancy.

The redeployment test

Before making a role redundant, an employer is expected to have genuinely considered whether the employee could reasonably be redeployed into another role in the business, or a related entity — even a role different from their current one, provided it's broadly suitable. Whether redeployment was "reasonable" depends on factors like the employee's skills and qualifications, and whether a suitable vacancy actually existed at the time, not hypothetically.

When a redundancy isn't genuine

Common red flags include: someone else being hired to do essentially the same job shortly afterwards; the "redundancy" really being a way to remove a specific individual without a real operational reason (sometimes called a sham redundancy); or the employer simply not following the consultation steps required under an applicable award or agreement. Where a redundancy isn't genuine, it may be able to be challenged as an unfair dismissal instead of accepted as redundancy.

What to do if you're not sure

You generally have 21 days from your termination date to lodge an unfair dismissal claim with the Fair Work Commission, so it's worth moving quickly if you think something's off. Start by gathering your termination letter, any correspondence about the decision, your position description, and anything showing whether (or how) you were consulted. Getting advice early matters here more than almost anywhere else in the process, given how short the window is.

Disclaimer: this guide explains the general legal test for a genuine redundancy and is not legal advice. Whether a specific redundancy is genuine depends on your individual facts — for an assessment of your situation, contact the Fair Work Ombudsman or a qualified employment lawyer.
